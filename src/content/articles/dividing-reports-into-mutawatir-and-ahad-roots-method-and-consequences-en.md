## Dividing Reports into Mutawatir and Ahad: Roots, Method, and Consequences

The division of reports into *mutawatir* (mass-transmitted) and *ahad* (solitary) is one of the most consequential issues in the intellectual structure of the Islamic sciences, because of its serious methodological effects on creed, on the standing of the Prophetic Sunnah, and on the relationship between reason and revelation. The division spread so widely in later works of legal theory and hadith terminology that it came to be treated as an established hadith principle. The article argues, however, that careful study shows the terms themselves to be old while the meanings and conditions attached to them are later developments, born in a context of theological disputation rather than in the environment of hadith criticism. It sets out to examine the division's historical origins, the epistemic foundations on which it rests, the position of the hadith imams, its practical effects on creed, and the Sunni method of dealing with reports.

## Historical Origins

The division, with its well-known conditions, did not emerge in the school of the hadith scholars. It arose in the Mu'tazili theological milieu, as part of a project that split religion into foundations (beliefs) and branches (rulings), required certainty in matters of belief, and rejected reports that, in their view, did not yield certainty. *Tawatur* was therefore not, for them, a description of how transmission actually occurred, but an exclusionary tool for deciding which texts to accept and which to reject. This explains why the division in this form is absent from the age of the Companions, the Successors, and the early hadith imams, even though the Prophetic message was complete in that age and the report of a trustworthy narrator was accepted as proof without regard to the number of transmitters.

## The Illusion That Creedal Evidence Is Mere "Information"

A flawed premise among the theologians was the belief that revelation, in matters of belief, consists merely of reports accepted by submission and containing no rational arguments. This drove them to look for an "independent reason" on which to found belief. The article counters that this contradicts the plain Qur'an: revelation addressed disbelievers and polytheists before believers, established its case through rational and innate proofs, and spoke to heart and mind together. Separating reason from revelation, and then making reason its judge, was the root that generated a chain of artificial divisions, foremost among them the division of reports into mutawatir and ahad with a creedal function.

## Clarifying the Question of "Yielding Knowledge"

The theologians did not reject the solitary report altogether. They held that it yields probability (*zann*) rather than certainty, and that probability is not accepted in matters of belief. The article identifies two methodological problems here. First, *zann* in the terminology of legal theorists is not doubt; it is the preponderant view supported by contextual indicators, and yet it was excluded from creed even though most of the Shari'ah's practical rulings rest on preponderant probability. Second, certainty in the Salafi method is not a mathematical property belonging to one type of report. It is an epistemic state reached through the accumulation of evidence, the presence of supporting indicators, and the Ummah's acceptance of a report. For this reason the hadith scholars held that a solitary report can yield knowledge when supported by such indicators, which dismantles the theological division from within.

## Critique of the Theological Conditions of Tawatur

The theologians stipulated conditions for a report to be mutawatir, among them a large number of narrators at every level, the impossibility of their colluding on a lie, and the report's being grounded in sense perception. These conditions, the article argues, were not derived from an inductive survey of narrations, are not supported by anything transmitted from the early generations, and are not met by any Prophetic hadith, not even the most famous. Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i confronted these conditions in *al-Risalah* and *Jima' al-'Ilm*, refuting them at the root and showing that the basis of acceptance is the narrators' integrity and accuracy, not their number or a bare claim that collusion is impossible.

## The Position of the Hadith Imams

The hadith scholars accepted reports on the basis of a sound and connected chain, the integrity and accuracy of narrators, a sound text, the absence of irregularity (*shudhudh*) and hidden defect (*'illah*), and corroboration where needed. They drew no distinction in argumentation between law and creed, and so they relied without hesitation on authentic solitary reports in the areas of the divine attributes, intercession, and the Last Day. Ibn Hajar al-'Asqalani, in *Nukhbat al-Fikar*, tried to refine the terminology and bring it closer to the hadith environment, dividing ahad into *mashhur*, *'aziz*, and *gharib* without making it a tool for rejecting creedal reports. Yet the wide circulation of his book fixed the terminology in later consciousness, sometimes loaded with its theological implications and without awareness of its history.

## Tawatur as a Phenomenon, Not an Exclusionary Condition

The early generations did not deny the existence of widely circulated reports with many chains; they accepted what was later called "thematic *tawatur*" (*al-tawatur al-ma'nawi*). What they rejected was making it a prerequisite for accepting the truth or a gateway for rejecting authentic texts. For them, *tawatur* was a description that follows, not a condition that precedes.

## Serious Practical Consequences

Among the gravest effects of the division was that groups of theologians rejected authentic hadiths about the divine attributes, such as the hadiths of the Descent and of the Vision of Allah, and rejected reports on intercession and the Last Day, not because of any defect in the chain but merely because they were "ahad." Yet the early generations received these matters with acceptance, and the imams of the Sunnah, such as Ahmad ibn Hanbal and others, relied on them without distinguishing between creed and law.

## Dismantling the Claim That "Beliefs Are Taken Only from Certainty"

Dividing religion into beliefs equated with certainty and rulings equated with probability is, the article argues, a later and non-Salafi division. Many practical rulings are founded on creedal principles, and many matters of belief were established by solitary reports that the Ummah received with acceptance. The early generations did not ask whether a report was ahad or mutawatir. They asked: Is it authentic? Does it contradict what is clear and decisive?

## Conclusion

The problem with dividing reports into mutawatir and ahad lies not in the description itself but in loading it with a function the early generations never knew: controlling the domain of creed and rejecting authentic texts on the claim that they do not yield certainty. The method of the Salaf and Ahl al-Sunnah wa al-Jama'ah does not require philosophical certainty. It relies on sound transmission, correct understanding, accumulated evidence, and the Ummah's acceptance, a method the article considers firmer and truer in representing both revelation and reality.
