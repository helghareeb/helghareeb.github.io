## The Mizan Series: How to Weigh Doubts — Article Eight

## Denying Sanctity and Justifying Desecration

In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful.

Praise be to Allah, and blessings and peace be upon the Messenger of Allah, his family, his Companions, and those who follow him.

A discussion may begin with a question about the source of sanctity, then move to a judgment about how one may treat what others revere. Someone may say: “I see no basis for this sanctity, so what prevents me from insulting its symbol or treating it with contempt?” Here a question arises that differs from investigating whether a belief is true: **Is merely disbelieving in the sanctity of something sufficient to make every action toward it acceptable?**

This article examines that transition when it occurs. It does not attribute it to everyone who adopts a social explanation of religion, nor does it quote a particular statement from a specific speaker. Someone may disagree with Muslims in matters of belief yet reject mistreatment of their copies of the Quran and their mosques, out of regard for their rights and a commitment to justice and good treatment. Fairness requires acknowledging this distinction before criticizing the claim.

## First: What Follows from Disagreement in Belief?

Someone who says that he does not believe a place or a book to be sacred has stated his position on a religious claim. Saying that he is entitled to desecrate that place or book, however, is a separate judgment requiring independent justification. The two statements do not necessarily follow from one another: evaluating an action also involves its intention, the harm it causes, and the rights of other people associated with it.

Even someone who considers certain values and symbols to originate in society is not thereby bound to regard them as having no value or deserving no protection. Many conventions that people establish to organize their relationships entail obligations that they acknowledge. It is therefore wrong to attribute permission for every violation to someone merely because he explains the origin of reverence socially.

Conversely, when this explanation is used to justify insult, we must examine the additional premise: Why should a lack of belief in something’s sanctity authorize every kind of treatment of it? A person may not share someone else’s attachment to a family keepsake yet still understand the wrongness of destroying it to humiliate its owner. This example illustrates how one can reject insult despite differing evaluations; it does not equate the standing of a keepsake with that of revelation.

## Second: Refraining from Insult Does Not Mean Endorsing a Belief

The Quran establishes a clear principle that prevents us from confusing rejection of a belief with unrestricted insult. Allah says, in meaning: “Do not insult those whom they invoke besides Allah, lest they insult Allah in hostility without knowledge. Thus We have made the deeds of every community seem attractive to them. Then to their Lord is their return, and He will inform them of what they used to do” [Al-An‘am 6:108].

The Quran, which invalidates worship of anyone other than Allah, here forbids insulting what the polytheists worship and explains the consequence: they may retaliate by insulting Allah in hostility and ignorance. Ibn Kathir explains this prohibition in terms of the greater harm to which the action leads.[^1] Refraining from insult did not endorse their worship, nor was judging it false sufficient to permit every means of confronting it.

This has an important implication for dialogue. A Muslim can explain why a belief is wrong and establish his argument against it while observing what Allah commands in his speech and conduct. Likewise, we do not require someone who disagrees with us to believe in the Quran as a condition for refraining from desecrating a copy of it. Discussing belief has its own evidence; discussing the act of insult has its own questions.

Considering consequences does not mean that an offender can force silence about every criticism by threatening a bad response. Rather, we distinguish presenting an argument from verbal abuse and weigh the method and its consequences, while maintaining each person’s responsibility for his own actions. Explaining a foreseeable cause of escalation does not exonerate someone who commits aggression.

## Third: Describing the Material Does Not Fully Describe the Action

A copy of the Quran may be described as “paper and ink,” with the implication that desecrating it is no more than handling ordinary material. But describing the material does not encompass the significance of the action. A sheet of paper may bear words, a covenant, or a message; the meaning of how it is treated varies according to what it carries and the context in which the action occurs.

A Muslim reveres a copy of the Quran because it contains the words of Allah. He does not believe that paper and ink possess an independent power to benefit or harm. Nevertheless, its being a physical object does not negate its sanctity under Islamic law. Describing the medium that carries the words is not enough to count as examining the source or standing of those words.

To assess a particular action, we need outward evidence: the actor’s own statement, how the act is presented, and any accompanying expressions or targeting. Damage to a copy does not by itself establish an intention to insult. It may happen accidentally. We may neither claim knowledge of every person’s inner intentions nor disregard context and assign the same judgment to every case.

Likewise, deliberately insulting a religious symbol does not prove that the aggressor believes it to be sacred. He may intend to hurt its believers while denying its sanctity. When established, the intention shows that he is dealing with a significance whose effect on others is understood. Describing the act as merely handling material is therefore insufficient.

## Fourth: Between Questions, Criticism, and Incitement

A questioner is entitled to understand what troubles him. A critic may present what he regards as a strong objection to a text or interpretation. The true character of his speech cannot be determined merely by the fact that some listeners feel hurt: a serious objection may distress someone unfamiliar with it. We therefore examine the content, the accuracy of quotation, the reasoning employed, and whether the speech examines a claim or targets its adherents for humiliation.

At the same time, insult does not become scholarly inquiry merely by being called criticism. If discussion of texts is replaced by gestures or expressions intended to show contempt, we may identify that conduct according to its reality and the evidence surrounding it. Incitement to harm people or their property adds a further element that must not be concealed beneath the heading of intellectual disagreement.

This distinction preserves the proper space for serious criticism, people’s rights, and the sanctity of sacred things for those who believe in them. It also prevents us from treating every mistake in expression as intentional aggression or removing all moral responsibility from an action merely by classifying it as an opinion.

## Fifth: Justice in Responding Is Part of Revering Religion

Allah says, in meaning: “O you who believe, stand firm for Allah as witnesses in justice. Do not let hatred of a people lead you to be unjust. Be just; that is nearer to piety. Be mindful of Allah. Allah is fully aware of what you do” [Al-Ma’idah 5:8].

Al-Sa‘di explains that hatred of an opponent does not justify abandoning justice toward him, and that whatever truth he brings is accepted because it is true.[^2] This requires us not to generalize an offender’s act to everyone sharing his country or religion, not to attribute to anyone a statement he did not make, and not to make anger an excuse for lying or injustice. The Prophet, blessings and peace be upon him, said: “Gentleness is never present in anything without adorning it, and is never removed from anything without disfiguring it.”[^3] Gentleness does not prevent clear condemnation or a strong argument. It disciplines both, so that the response explains the truth rather than becoming an arena for fresh wrongdoing. Revering the words of Allah also appears in obeying His commands when we are angry on their behalf.

The connection between ideas and actions remains worthy of examination, but only to the extent supported by evidence. An idea may be used to justify a harmful act; we criticize that use when it appears. Claiming that everyone who holds the idea will endorse that act, or intended to pave the way for it, requires evidence that mere disagreement does not supply. Equally, an idea is not invalidated simply because someone has misused it. We examine its evidence, then examine how it is being used to support an action.

## A Conclusion for Young Readers

Do not reduce discussion of sanctity to two options: either your opponent believes what you believe, or he is permitted to insult it. People can differ in belief while remaining committed to justice and good treatment. When someone says that something is merely paper or stone, ask about the significance and context of the action without claiming knowledge of intentions. Distinguish questions, criticism, and contempt, then adhere to this standard yourself when responding. Defending sacred things requires sound evidence and disciplined character; it is not strengthened by making an opponent answer for consequences that do not follow from his words.

All praise belongs to Allah, Lord of the worlds.
