## The Mizan Series: How to Weigh Doubts — Article Nine

## The Mushaf Is Not Mere Paper and Ink

All praise belongs to Allah.

When a copy of the Mushaf is attacked, someone may say: only pages and print were burned, so why all this anger? A comparison may be added between anger for the Mushaf and silence about a hungry or oppressed human being. There is a correct part in this argument that we should not lose: paper is a material that can perish, and relieving the needy is an obligation that no war of words may make us forget. But does it follow that insulting the Mushaf is an act devoid of meaning, or that respecting it conflicts with mercy towards people?

The flaw begins when describing a thing in terms of its material turns into a judgement that exhausts its whole value. The answer therefore needs to distinguish between the paper and what is written on it, between forms that look alike outwardly but differ in their aims and context, and between safeguarding the Qur’an and neglecting human rights.

## What Is Missing from the Description “Mere Paper”?

The Mushaf is written on paper, but the Muslim does not revere it because paper is a rare kind of material; he reveres it for what it carries of the Speech of Allah. The existence of a physical carrier for a text does not make its content equal to that carrier, just as a letter bearing fateful news does not become worthless because its material is ink and paper. The example shows the inadequacy of the material description; it does not equate the Speech of Allah with the speech of human beings. Moreover, understanding what an insult means does not depend on sharing the belief of the one insulted. A person who does not believe in the Qur’an can understand why choosing the Mushaf for a public attack is a message directed at Muslims. This does not prove that he acknowledges its divine source; it may be enough that he knows its standing among them and then targets that standing in order to humiliate.

In return, it is not right to attribute an intent to insult to everyone who has destroyed a sheet containing Qur’an, or to treat filming alone as conclusive proof of what is in people’s hearts. An act is understood from the whole of its indications: what happened? Why was this particular object chosen? What words accompanied it? What is the evident context? Fairness requires that we neither cancel the significance of these indications nor go beyond them to claims without evidence.

## Why Did ‘Uthman Order Pages and Copies of the Mushaf to Be Burned?

An objector may cite what is established from ‘Uthman ibn ‘Affan, may Allah be pleased with him, and say: if burning the Mushaf is an insult, how could ‘Uthman have ordered it? The answer is that the report itself explains the reason for the act and its aim.

Al-Bukhari narrates that Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman, may Allah be pleased with him, feared that the people would differ in reciting the Qur’an, so he asked ‘Uthman to remedy the matter. ‘Uthman sent to Hafsah, may Allah be pleased with her, requesting the sheets so that they could copy them into codices and then return them to her, and he assigned a group of the Companions to this task. After the copying, he returned the sheets to Hafsah, sent the codices to the provinces, and ordered that all other sheets and codices be burned.[^1]

This is not a scene intended to erase the Qur’an or to belittle its people. It was a measure joined to copying the Qur’an, spreading its codices, and uniting the people upon them in order to ward off disagreement. To establish this meaning we do not need to claim that every sheet burned was worn out, or to assert a consensus that we have not verified; the context of the narration is enough to show the difference between this act and taking the Mushaf as an instrument of mockery.

There is another question: pages that have worn out or can no longer be used. Shaykh Ibn Baz permitted burning them or burying them in clean ground, to protect them from being treated with contempt.[^2] This is different from the event of compiling the codices, though it shares with it the point that burning does not carry a single ruling merely by its physical form. The difference is not reduced to an intention the doer claims; one also looks at the legitimate purpose, the nature of the action, and the context in which it takes place. Good intention alone does not make every means correct.

## The Preservation of the Qur’an Does Not Mean That Attacking It Is Harmless

Allah the Exalted says: “Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed, We will be its guardian” (Al-Hijr 15:9; translation of the meaning). Ibn Kathir explained His preservation here as protecting it from alteration and change.[^3] It is therefore not right to turn the promise to preserve the Qur’an into a promise that every copy of it will remain safe from damage, or into an argument that removes responsibility from the one who attacks a copy.

A copy may perish while the Qur’an remains preserved, recited and learned by people. But the survival of the text does not make destroying the copy permissible, nor does it negate what may accompany it: waste of property, harm, or deliberate contempt for sacred things. Saying that the aggressor cannot erase the Qur’an reassures the believer that his religion is preserved; it does not turn the aggressor’s attack into an innocent act.

Hence two things can be held together: we do not imagine that the fate of the Qur’an depends on the safety of one particular sheet, and we condemn the degradation of that sheet because of the Speech of Allah it carries. Trust in Allah’s promise does not cancel the duty of reverence, just as reverence does not need exaggeration that portrays every attack as if it were the end of the religion.

## And What About the Hungry and the Oppressed?

The question about the hungry is legitimate, and it may expose a real flaw if we find someone who spends his effort on public anger and then pays no attention to the right of a needy relative or a suffering neighbour. But the remedy for this flaw is to fulfil the neglected right, not to justify insulting the Qur’an. The Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, said: “Feed the hungry, visit the sick, and free the captive.”[^4] Care for human beings is part of the very religion we wish to honour; it is not a foreign demand competing with it.

Nor is the matter that time and money are without limits. Ordering priorities is a necessity, and urgent relief may take precedence over joining a debate or commenting on a news item. Yet giving precedence to one deed at a particular time does not deny the ugliness of another act. We can condemn the insult, and then ask sincerely: what is the more obligatory deed now? Where is our capacity most beneficial? Pointing to the shortcomings of some defenders of the Mushaf does not, by itself, decide the case they are defending.

A person may be right in condemning an act and wrong in neglecting another duty. So we affirm what is right and ask him to repair the shortcoming, instead of making one of them a pretext for erasing the other.

## From Defending the Mushaf to Keeping Company with the Qur’an

Our bond with the Qur’an is not complete if we become angry when it is insulted and then abandon it once the news dies down. The Exalted says: “A blessed Book which We have revealed to you, that they might reflect upon its verses and that those of understanding would be reminded” (Sad 38:29; translation of the meaning). Revering it extends to reciting it, reflecting on it, learning its guidance, and holding oneself to account for what one takes from it and what one leaves.

The more this zeal turns into worship, knowledge, and good deeds, the truer its effect on the one who has it. Contenting oneself with chasing scenes of provocation, however, may exhaust one’s attention without increasing one’s share of the Qur’an. What is required is not to abolish condemnation, but that it be accompanied by work that builds the bond with what we condemn being attacked.

## A Summary for Young People

The Mushaf is paper that carries the Speech of Allah, so describing its material does not exhaust the explanation of its standing. Destroying pages in order to safeguard them differs from degrading them, as becomes clear from the purpose, the context, and the nature of the act. The preservation of the Qur’an does not justify attacking its copies, and revering it does not exempt anyone from feeding the hungry and supporting the oppressed. So combine a clear position with a sound ordering of duties, and make your defence of the Qur’an the beginning of more recitation of it, more reflection on it, and more acting upon it.
