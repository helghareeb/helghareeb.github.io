## The Presence of Preachers on Social Media Platforms

## Between the Interest of Da'wah and the Risks of Negative Influence

The article opens by arguing that the presence of preachers on social media is no longer a side issue or a marginal choice. These platforms have become the most present and influential public space, a main source for shaping awareness, forming perceptions, and directing behavior. This raises a legitimate question: is the presence of preachers there a gain for da'wah, or do its harms outweigh its benefits? The article holds that this question cannot be answered in absolute terms or settled by impression. It requires balanced scholarly consideration that takes into account the fiqh of means, weighs benefits against harms, and keeps in view the higher objectives of the Shari'ah and the consequences of actions.

## The Basic Legitimacy of Presence

In principle, the means of da'wah are not sought for their own sake but for the ends they serve. The scholars have established that means take the rulings of their ends: whatever is a means to a legitimate end, and does not contain a preponderant harm, is permissible in general. Throughout its history da'wah has used the means of each age: the pulpit, circles of knowledge, travel in pursuit of hadith, and scholarly correspondence. Engaging with available means was never a departure from the methodology, so long as it was governed by knowledge and scrupulousness. From this angle, using social media to convey knowledge and give reminders is not to be forbidden in itself, but neither is it to be accepted without qualification.

## Possible Benefits for Da'wah

The article does not deny that there are real benefits. Da'wah is no longer confined to one region or a narrow social circle, and the message now reaches groups that traditional means never reached. In a time when unqualified people put themselves forward to speak about religion, the presence of a specialist preacher helps reduce the space of ignorance and correct some concepts, even if not perfectly. And not everyone is a student of knowledge; most people still need reminding, alerting, and connecting to the great values of faith. Yet these benefits, important as they are, cannot be understood in isolation from the harms that stand against them.

## The Accompanying Methodological Risks

The danger of these platforms is that they do not run on the logic of knowledge but on the logic of attraction and spread. Several ills follow from this. Da'wah turns into "content": algorithms impose their own logic of excitement and brevity, pushing religious discourse, without anyone intending it, toward harmful oversimplification, constant controversy, or treating issues by the logic of engagement rather than the logic of careful verification. Knowledge is flattened and fragmented: Islamic knowledge is a cumulative structure that needs grounding and gradual progression, while the platforms run on short clips and fragmentary messages, which produces fragile understanding and tempts the unqualified to put themselves forward.

The article singles out the making of the "influencer preacher" as one of the most dangerous problems. Popular acceptance can quietly become an unspoken criterion of what is correct, and the pressure of followers can, consciously or unconsciously, shape the choice of topics and the way they are presented. Related to this is a disturbance in how truth is received: the truth is sometimes measured by the number of views, and popularity is relied upon instead of verification. This is a serious inversion of the method by which right is distinguished from wrong.

## The Criterion Between Benefit and Harm

The right question, the article says, is not "Should the preacher take part or not?" but the more precise question, "When is participation disciplined and fruitful?" The benefit is preponderant, in general, when a number of conditions are met: a clear scholarly identity, with the aim being to teach people rather than to manufacture a presence; distinguishing between discourse addressed to the general public and discourse addressed to students of knowledge; freedom from the pressure of the audience, without making engagement the standard of evaluation; keeping one's scholarly frame of reference outside the platform, not inside it; and readiness to reduce one's presence or withdraw if harm prevails or the balance is disturbed. Without these conditions, presence turns from a means of da'wah into a source of scholarly and methodological depletion.

## Caring for the Preacher's Heart Is a Condition of Sound Da'wah, Not an Extra

One of the most dangerous effects of immersion in social media is that the preacher may, without noticing, become occupied with people instead of with himself, with being present before people instead of being present with Allah, and with visible impact instead of inner building. Da'wah is in origin an act of worship before it is a communication activity, and its effect is sound only when the heart that carries it is sound. The scholars have established that the soundness of the outward is a branch of the soundness of the inward, and that a corrupt intention corrupts the deed even if people see great benefit in it. Constant preoccupation with the platforms, following engagement, and measuring impact by numbers can therefore lead to a hidden erosion of sincerity, to hardness of heart, or to slackness in worship and in seeking knowledge. That is a price no number of followers can make up for.

Part of the understanding of da'wah is to realize that constant presence is not a virtue in itself, and that a disciplined absence may at times be purer for the heart, safer for one's religion, and closer to a sound intention. The Salaf, who had the greatest impact of all people, were the most fearful for themselves of fame and the most keen on obscurity, despite the great benefit they brought to people. Among the conditions that must not be neglected, then, are: setting fixed time for worship, self-accounting, and seeking knowledge away from the platforms; reviewing one's intention regularly and questioning oneself honestly; and remembering that benefiting people does not substitute for a sound heart. The preacher who reforms people and neglects himself may, Allah forbid, become a bridge over which people cross to salvation while he himself is in danger of falling.

## Conclusion

The presence of preachers on social media is neither pure good nor pure evil. It is a dangerous means that requires deep understanding, continual watchfulness, and honest review. The error does not lie in using it as such, but in the absence of discipline, the shifting of priorities, and turning the means into an end. The essential question, therefore, should not only be: "Am I using these platforms to serve da'wah, or am I reshaping da'wah to serve the logic of the platforms?" A deeper question must be added to it: "Is da'wah still bringing me closer to Allah, or have I begun drawing closer to people and further from Him?" That, the article concludes, is the true measure.
