## On the Rules of Takhrij and Isnad Study

## Methodological Foundations and Practical Guidelines for the Contemporary Researcher

The article presents takhrij (locating and documenting a hadith in its sources) and the study of chains of transmission as among the most important hadith sciences and the most closely tied to scholarly verification. Takhrij is, in reality, the applied side of the sciences of hadith terminology, narrator criticism and hidden defects. Its value lies in distinguishing the authentic from the weak, showing where narrations differ, uncovering both apparent and hidden defects, and then bringing all of this to bear on the research and its results. With the abundance of printed works and software today, recurring methodological errors have appeared, the most serious being reliance on intermediary sources, restriction to a single route, and presenting takhrij as a "formal documentation" exercise in which the rules of the hadith craft are not actually used. Hence the need to set down governing rules that preserve the seriousness of hadith research and tie the researcher to the method settled by the experts over the centuries.

## Takhrij as the Fruit of the Hadith Sciences

Takhrij is not purely a matter of documentation; it is where knowledge of terminology, the rules of narrator criticism and the subtleties of hidden defects meet in practice. Its aim is not merely to find where a hadith occurs, but to build a scholarly picture of the hadith's routes and points of origin, the differences in its wordings and chains, the grounds for preferring one narration over another when they conflict, and whether a judgment on the narration can be reached or approximated. Careful takhrij is therefore part of building the scholarly argument of a study, not a documentation step appended at the end.

## Gathering the Routes: A Condition for Verifying Authenticity

A golden rule in this field is that the five conditions of the authentic hadith can usually be verified only by gathering its routes. Continuity of the chain, the uprightness and precision of the narrators, the absence of anomaly and freedom from hidden defect cannot be properly established except by surveying the narrations and comparing their paths. Gathering routes is thus the key to uncovering hidden defects, for many errors appear only when narrations are compared with one another: a narrator may connect what was transmitted as mursal, attribute to the Prophet ﷺ what was transmitted as a Companion's statement, insert or invert material, or add something in which he contradicts the leading memorizers. Neglecting to gather routes does not produce a merely formal shortcoming; it leads to a real flaw in understanding and judgment.

## Original and Intermediary Sources

An original source in takhrij is any book in which the author narrates the hadith with his own chain from his teachers back to the Prophet ﷺ. This does not depend on the subject of the book, which may be jurisprudence, exegesis, history or language; what matters is the presence of the chain and the author's own connected route. An intermediary (secondary) source is a book that transmits hadiths from the original sources without a chain of the author's own. Such works can be useful for pointing to where a hadith occurs, for gathering routes, or for transmitting critical observations, but they are not the primary basis for takhrij when the original is available.

The firm rule of citation is that one does not cite an intermediary source when the original source exists and can be accessed, because the original is the place of verification and is what study and comparison rely on. The only recognized exceptions are when the original is lost or unavailable, or when the circulating copy of the original has a defect, such as an obvious scribal error, a lacuna or confusion, that requires the researcher to seek support from the intermediary. When the original is lost and the researcher must quote through an intermediary, the citation should show the path precisely, in the form: "Reported by so-and-so (the original source), as cited in the book of so-and-so (the intermediary source)." This makes the path of transmission explicit, secures the attribution of the words to their speaker, and keeps the difference between the original and the transmitter clear.

## Levels of Citation

The manner of citation varies with the scholarly context and the nature of the research. The most common are three levels. Extended citation names the book, then the chapter (or its equivalent), then volume and page, and the hadith number where one exists; it is best suited to scholarly research and to studies in which a judgment rests on differences between narrations. Intermediate citation usually gives only volume, page and hadith number, without chapter details, especially when access is controlled by a recognized numbering. Brief citation simply attributes the hadith to whoever reported it, such as "reported by al-Bukhari" or "reported by Muslim," sometimes with the hadith number when a reliable numbering is used, and is suitable where there is no need to specify the location in detail. The article also advises avoiding the old abbreviation symbols in contemporary scholarly writing, because stating the compiler's or the book's name is clearer, less open to confusion and more conducive to sound documentation.

## Ordering Sources in Citation

The order of sources in takhrij is not decoration but part of scholarly precision: it shows the value of the original, makes it easier for the reader to check, and prevents mixing original sources with intermediaries. Two recognized methods have become established. The first begins with the Six Books in the agreed order, al-Bukhari, then Muslim, then Abu Dawud, then al-Tirmidhi, then al-Nasa'i, then Ibn Majah, followed by the remaining works according to the aim of the takhrij and the nature of the research. The second, common among the early scholars and deserving attention, orders all sources by the death dates of their compilers from earliest to latest, since this reveals the layers of transmission and the path by which a narration passed through the compilations, and is most useful when gathering routes and tracing the common link of a hadith.

Contemporary takhrij has also widely adopted the "Nine Books" as a starting core for general takhrij: the Six Books together with the Muwatta' of Malik, the Musnad of Ahmad and the Sunan of al-Darimi. The common order when citing them is Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim, Sunan Abi Dawud, Jami' al-Tirmidhi, Sunan al-Nasa'i, Sunan Ibn Majah, Muwatta' Malik, Musnad Ahmad and Sunan al-Darimi. This order helps unify the method of reference within a study and eases reading, while ordering by death dates remains a well-founded method for precise studies.

## Practical Notes That Keep Takhrij Consistent

The article adds several practical cautions. One should distinguish al-Nasa'i's Minor Sunan (al-Mujtaba), which is among the Six Books, from his Major Sunan (al-Sunan al-Kubra), and it is advisable when needed to cite both, since the Minor is a selection from the Major, and the Major may contain material not found in al-Mujtaba or may clarify an aspect of the narration. In the Musnad of Ahmad, which is arranged by Companions, the equivalent of "book and chapter" is the name of the Companion's musnad or group of musnads, which makes locating the hadith easier. In some works numbers are repeated or disordered in certain editions, such as al-Mu'jam al-Kabir of al-Tabarani and al-Ahadith al-Mukhtarah of al-Diya' al-Maqdisi, so it is advisable to cite volume, page and number together to avoid confusion. Finally, the term "books of rulings" (kutub al-ahkam) needs to be used precisely: it refers to works that gather hadiths on legal rulings arranged by the chapters of jurisprudence for the purpose of argument, not works originally composed to issue judgments of authenticity and weakness on hadiths. Some of their authors do add hadith-critical remarks on chains and texts according to their method, as seen in some well-known books of this genre. Confusing the purpose of legal compilation with that of pure hadith criticism leads the researcher into confusion about the function of the work and how to benefit from it in takhrij.

## Using Digital Hadith Encyclopedias

There is no doubt that hadith encyclopedias and computer programs have brought the sources of the Sunnah much closer and made wordings and routes easier to reach. Benefiting from them, however, requires methodological understanding that distinguishes the tool from the result, and speed of access from soundness of verification. Misuse of these tools has led to recurring errors in contemporary research:

- **A narrow search scope.** Some researchers search only within a limited set of famous books, whereas sound verification requires building a wide search scope that includes, as far as possible, all available hadith collections with chains: Sahihs, Sunans, Musnads, Mu'jams and hadith booklets (ajza'). Restricting the search may miss genuine routes and defeats the purpose of gathering routes.
- **Mixing original and intermediary in a single citation.** A researcher may find the hadith in its original source and then, in the same place, cite both the original and an intermediary that transmits the same route. The intermediary should be mentioned only when there is a scholarly need, such as an additional benefit, a difference of wording or a ruling by one of the imams; placing it alongside the original as an equal reference for the same route weakens the takhrij and confuses the reader.
- **Not distinguishing the author of an atraf work from the author of the original.** Some researchers attribute rulings or chains quoted in an atraf (index of hadith openings) work to the author of the atraf himself, although his role is usually referral, compilation and indication, not independent narration and judgment. One must understand each book's method before quoting from it and attribute a statement to its real author.
- **Neglecting intermediary sources where they are needed.** At the opposite extreme, some researchers dismiss intermediary sources altogether as mere conduits of no value, although several of these works have preserved chains and routes from lost books and sometimes transmit hadith texts available today only through them. Ignoring them when the original is lost leads to the loss of genuine routes that may strengthen a hadith or reveal its defect.
- **Passing over results from works without chains without scholarly examination.** Search results may show texts in books of jurisprudence, exegesis or history that are not themselves works with chains, and some researchers hastily ignore them. Yet such works may contain valuable critical material: imams' identifications of defects, preferences between conflicting narrations, references to other routes, or rulings not found in pure takhrij works. The sounder method is to examine them with a critical eye and then trace them back to their original sources as far as possible.
- **Neglecting intermediaries for correcting scribal and copying errors.** Typographical and digital errors in narrators' names and in texts are not rare, and correcting them requires careful comparison of the available copies. One useful method is to compare what appears in the original text with what scholars have quoted in carefully edited intermediary sources, since their quotation may be more accurate and can help correct names or distinguish similar ones.

The conclusion of this section is that technology serves knowledge but does not replace the trained faculty of hadith expertise. A program points to where a text is, but it does not give the researcher an understanding of how to order sources, does not uncover hidden defects for him, and does not distinguish the original from the intermediary. Sound contemporary takhrij requires combining skill in computer searching with the early scholars' rules for gathering routes, understanding the methods of compilers, and precision in citation and verification.

## Conclusion

These rules show that takhrij is not a secondary task but the core of the hadith method, through which the accuracy of narration is established, its variations understood and its sound distinguished from its unsound. Gathering routes is the cornerstone for uncovering hidden defects and preferring between narrations. Careful handling of original and intermediary sources is a scholarly necessity, and the order of sources in citation is not a matter of formatting but part of trustworthiness and precision, whether one adopts the order of the Six Books and the Nine Books to serve the reader, or the early scholars' ordering by death dates to show the layers and path of transmission. In this way takhrij moves from the form of "documentation" to the reality of "research," and from merely listing sources to a scholarly craft connected to its foundations that absorbs the resources of the age without surrendering the demands of verification.
