## The Mizan Series: How to Weigh Doubts, Article One

## Narrator Criticism and Text Criticism in Weighing Doubts

A Muslim may hear a doubt connected to a prophetic hadith or a historical event and find in front of him three distinct things: a transmitted report, an interpretation of that report, and then a sweeping verdict on the Sunnah or on Muslim history. These must be kept apart. The report may not be established at all; it may be established but misunderstood; or part of what is said may be correct while the conclusion built on it is false. Hence the need to learn how to examine a claim before either accepting it or hastily rejecting it. This is the purpose of the Mizan ("Balance") series: to set out the principles of verification and apply them to contemporary doubts, with care for texts and their sources and fairness in presenting views. The series begins with the hadith scholars' method of criticizing reports, showing how they examined both the transmitter and the text, while preserving the difference between a reader's awareness of these principles and a specialist's expertise in the fine details of hadith and its hidden defects.

## The Isnad: The Path to Verifying Attribution

The isnad is the chain of narrators through whom a report was transmitted. Examining it is not a matter of listing names; it includes the continuity of transmission, knowledge of the narrators' truthfulness and precision, and distinguishing what they memorized accurately from what they confused. A man may be truthful yet err in memory, and the narrators may be reliable while the route between them is broken. 'Abd Allah ibn al-Mubarak said, "The isnad is part of the religion; were it not for the isnad, anyone would say whatever he wished." The article stresses that these are the words of a Muslim scholar, recorded in the introduction to Sahih Muslim, not a hadith of the Prophet ﷺ. They show that verifying attribution is part of preserving the religion: a statement does not become proof merely because it is presented well or prefaced with "the Messenger of Allah said."

The same place in Muslim's introduction gives an example of how precise this scrutiny was. Ibn al-Mubarak was asked about a report and asked who narrated it. He was told Shihab ibn Khirash, and said, "Reliable; from whom?" Then al-Hajjaj ibn Dinar was named, and he said, "Reliable; from whom?" When the questioner reached the attribution to the Prophet ﷺ, Ibn al-Mubarak pointed out the gap between al-Hajjaj and the Prophet ﷺ. He was not satisfied with two reliable narrators; he examined the whole route by which the report was attributed to its source. For general verification, this teaches us to ask for the source and then read what is in it: is it a direct quotation or a report about someone else? Does the author present the report as accepted, or cite it in order to criticize it? Is the attribution to its speaker established? Simply finding a phrase in some book answers none of these questions.

## Authenticity Covers Both Chain and Text

Ibn al-Salah, in his 'Ulum al-Hadith, defines the sahih hadith as "the connected hadith whose chain is continuous through the transmission of upright, precise narrators from upright, precise narrators to its end, and which is neither anomalous (shadhdh) nor defective (mu'allal)." The absence of anomaly and hidden defect is thus part of the very definition of authenticity, not an optional extra that can be ignored once the narrators' names are known. He states plainly that it may be said, "This hadith has an authentic chain, yet it is not authentic, because it is anomalous or defective." In the same context, however, he notes that when a reliable compiler declares a chain authentic and mentions no defect, the apparent implication of his practice is that he judges the hadith itself to be authentic. The distinction between the two expressions therefore does not entitle a reader to imagine a defect in every hadith, or to reject the critics' rulings on the basis of a mere possibility for which there is no evidence.

In his chapter on the defective hadith, Ibn al-Salah explains that a defect may lie in the chain or in the text, and that it is detected by gathering the routes, comparing the narrations and examining the memory and precision of the narrators. The problem may appear in a word added by one narrator, in connecting a report that the leading memorizers transmitted as broken, or in attributing to the Prophet ﷺ words that are properly preserved as someone else's. This comparison covers the narrations with their chains and wording, and cannot be reduced to examining each narrator in isolation. Portraying the hadith scholars as accepting any text so long as its narrators are reliable therefore does not describe their method as they themselves set it out. Conversely, not every solitary narration is an anomaly, and not every difference between two wordings is a damaging defect; one must examine the indicators, the points of agreement and difference, and the ability of the narrations to explain one another.

## Text Criticism Is a Discipline with Rules

Text criticism does not mean making what a reader finds familiar the standard for judging what is attributed to the Prophet ﷺ. A meaning may seem strange because of limited knowledge of the language, the context or the other relevant texts, just as it may seem strange because of a genuine flaw in the narration; only careful investigation distinguishes the two. One must therefore distinguish between what is rationally impossible and what is merely extraordinary: the fact that an event breaks the normal course of things is no argument for denying a miracle whose report is established. Likewise, an apparent conflict between two texts is not enough to declare one of them false before examining their meanings and the ways of reconciling them. Nor is it fair to ascribe to a hadith a meaning it does not bear and then treat an objection to that meaning as an attack on the hadith itself.

Ibn al-Salah describes the science of hidden defects as among the most subtle of the hadith sciences, undertaken by those with memorization, experience and penetrating understanding. Knowing the definition of a defect is not the same as being able to identify one in a specific hadith. The reader benefits from knowing these principles and then refers what puzzles him to specialists, asking them to explain the basis and evidence of their judgment. In contemporary academic research, the article notes, Jonathan Brown studied evidence that the early hadith critics practised content criticism and discussed the view that confines their work to chains, in his 2008 study "How We Know Early Ḥadīth Critics Did Matn Criticism and Why It's So Hard to Find" (Islamic Law and Society). The article treats this as a specific study with its own evidence and arguments, without turning one researcher's view into an academic consensus or making acceptance of the Sunnah depend on the testimony of modern studies.

## From Criticizing the Report to Examining the Argument

After a report has been verified, another question remains: does it actually prove the conclusion being drawn from it? A transmission may be sound and the reasoning faulty. This stage needs to be made visible so that the reader does not think that accepting a correct premise obliges him to accept everything built upon it. The article gives two hypothetical examples, not attributed in this wording to any particular person. If someone says that part of the manuscript heritage remains unpublished and therefore we have no reliable knowledge of our tradition, the proportion of published material first requires a count that defines the field and what counts as a manuscript; and the existence of unpublished sources does not by itself invalidate knowledge based on established sources. One examines the effect of new material when it appears, and what it adds or corrects. Similarly, if a writer calls the site of an ancient event by a modern name familiar to his readers, that alone is no proof that the event was fabricated. One asks whether the name comes from an ancient source claimed to be quoted verbatim or is a modern identification of the place, and whether the claim concerns the existence of the place or of a structure built there later. Distinguishing these possibilities is part of criticism, and confusing them can spoil the response itself.

The point is that we must weigh our answer with the same fairness we demand of the person raising the doubt. If we find no source for a report, we say its source has not been established for us, and we do not make the limits of our search into proof that it never happened. If we find evidence that it is false, we set out that evidence. And if part of the opponent's argument is correct, we acknowledge it and then examine the rest of his claim.

## Practical Questions Before Accepting or Rejecting a Claim

The article offers six ordered questions:

1. What exactly is the claim? Is it the attribution of a statement, the assertion of an event, the interpretation of a text, or a general verdict? The answer must address the claim actually made, not another one.
2. What is the source, and was it transmitted faithfully? Check the location and context of the words, distinguish the author's own words from those he quotes, and watch for abridgements that change the meaning.
3. Is the report established by the method appropriate to it? For hadith, refer to its documentation (takhrij), the scholars' ruling and its grounds; for historical reports, examine their sources and indicators, without assuming that methods of proof are identical in every field.
4. Has the text been understood in light of its language, context and related texts? The objection may target a misunderstanding, a truncated quotation or a wording not established in the narration.
5. Does the conclusion follow from its premises? Similarity alone does not prove borrowing, an individual's error does not entail the corruption of an entire discipline, and the occurrence of one event does not justify generalizing its ruling to all events.
6. How much has the evidence actually established, and what requires asking a specialist? Distinguish the established from the possible and the unknown, stop where one's knowledge ends, and do not let self-confidence replace proof.

These questions are a means of organizing one's thinking, not a substitute for learning the Shari'ah sciences or consulting their people. Their benefit is that the reader identifies where the difficulty lies, asks a precise question and understands the answer according to its evidence, instead of moving from a vague doubt to a vague reply.

## A Summary for Young People

If you hear a doubt, you are not obliged to answer it immediately. Define its meaning, ask for its source, and separate the soundness of the report from the soundness of the conclusion drawn from it. Beware of passing on, in defence of the religion, anything you have not verified, for honesty in transmission binds both those who agree and those who disagree. Learn from the method of the hadith scholars their care for attribution, wording and context, while recognizing the degree of specialization that judging narrations requires. Ask the people of knowledge about whatever is unclear to you, for suspending judgment until the evidence becomes clear is closer to the truth than a hasty answer.
