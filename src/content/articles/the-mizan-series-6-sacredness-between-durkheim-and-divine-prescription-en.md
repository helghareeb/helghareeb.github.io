## The Mizan Series: How to Weigh Doubts — Article Six

## Sacredness Between Durkheim and the Requirement of Revealed Authority

In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful.

All praise is due to Allah, and may blessings and peace be upon the Messenger of Allah, his family, his Companions, and those who follow him.

People differ in their reverence for places and objects. What the followers of one religion regard as sacred may appear to others as a building, a stone, or a river that stirs no particular feeling within them. From this observation arises an objection: society creates all sacredness, and a revered object derives its sanctity only from people's agreement about it. The name of the French sociologist Émile Durkheim is invoked to support this explanation, which is then applied to the Kaaba and the other sacred rites and symbols of Islam.

This claim is not answered by denying the influence of upbringing and environment on people's feelings, nor by asserting that every inherited form of reverence is valid. Rather, the response begins by distinguishing between how a person acquires a belief and the evidence for that belief's truth. We then clarify what a Muslim means by the sanctity of a place, so that the discussion addresses the position the Muslim actually holds, rather than some other meaning attributed to it.

### First: What Does Durkheim Say?

In *The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life*, Durkheim connects the sacred with the power a group experiences when it comes together, and with the symbols that express that power. He maintains that sacred character is not derived solely from an object's material properties, but is conferred upon it in collective life.[1] Thus, a summary of his view that the group plays a role in conferring sacredness has a basis in his theory, even though not every Arabic formulation in circulation is a literal translation of his words.

Fairness, however, requires that we not reduce his theory to a neutral description of people's rituals. He goes further, explaining the sacred principle as society in a transformed and personified form, and treating the gods as its symbolic expression.[2]

He therefore offers an explanation of the phenomenon's origin, rather than merely describing its effects. To engage with his account is to examine this explanation and its adequacy, not to deny what he said and then appeal to his name.

Nevertheless, his view remains a theory with its own premises and reasoning. Mentioning a great scholar's name is not enough to make a conclusion necessarily follow, just as our disagreement with him is not enough to dismiss everything he observed about a group's influence on its members' feelings and behavior.

### Second: The Origin of a Belief and the Evidence for Its Truth

A child learns the language, customs, and many of the beliefs of their community from their surroundings. What they learn may be true or false. Knowing the route by which a claim reached someone does not, by itself, settle the claim's merit. If a person learns an arithmetic truth from their family, the family's role in teaching it does not make its truth a creation of the family, nor does it dispense with the person's need to know its proof.

This example illustrates the logical distinction; it does not make religious and arithmetic propositions identical in the evidence they require. Asking why a belief is widespread is different from asking what evidence supports it. A belief may have both social causes for its spread and independent evidence for its truth, or it may spread despite weak evidence. Each particular case must therefore be examined.

Investigating the origin of a belief is not without value. It may reveal manipulation, indoctrination, or reliance on an untrustworthy source, thereby affecting our assessment of it. The error arises when a general observation, such as people's being influenced by their societies, is turned into a sweeping judgment that every religious claim is false. That conclusion goes beyond the premise and requires a further argument.

Likewise, the fact that followers of different religions differ over what they hold sacred demonstrates a difference in their beliefs. By itself, however, it does not establish that none of those beliefs could be true. Nor may a believer reverse the argument and treat the number of those who revere something, or the intensity of their feelings, as sufficient evidence for the validity of everything they revere.

### Third: The Source of Sanctity in Islam

What is meant here by the requirement of revealed authority (*al-tawqīf al-sharʿī*) is that singling out a place or object for religious merit, or assigning a particular act of worship to it, must rest on evidence from revelation. People have no authority to institute a new act of worship merely because they find its location pleasing or experience powerful feelings there. Ordinary respect for people, memories, and property, however, does not always amount to a claim of religious sacredness and should not all be treated under this same heading. Allah Most High says, in a rendering of the meaning: “That is so. And whoever honors the sacred rites and symbols of Allah, surely this is from the piety of hearts” [al-Ḥajj 22:32]. And He, glorified is He, says, in a rendering of the meaning: “I have only been commanded to worship the Lord of this city, who has made it sacred, and to whom all things belong; and I have been commanded to be among those who submit to Him” [al-Naml 27:91].

The attribution of these sacred rites and symbols to Allah, and the explicit statement that He made the city sacred, identify the authority to which a Muslim refers. In the hadith narrated by Ibn ʿAbbās, may Allah be pleased with him and his father, the Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, said of Mecca: “Indeed, this is a city that Allah made sacred on the day He created the heavens and the earth, and it remains sacred by Allah's decree until the Day of Resurrection.”[3] In Islamic belief, this ruling does not await everyone's agreement, nor does it cease to apply because a visitor does not feel it.

This does not mean that stones possess an independent power to benefit or harm, or that sanctity is a material property measurable by a stone's weight or composition. It is an honor and a ruling bestowed by Allah, entailing acts and limits prescribed by Islamic law. Consequently, considering the Kaaba as a material building does not exhaust the question of its religious status, just as describing the physical material of a copy of the Qur'an does not, on its own, answer the question of the nature of the words written within it.

### Fourth: What Does ʿUmar's Statement at the Black Stone Show?

It is established that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, may Allah be pleased with him, kissed the Black Stone and said: “I know that you are a stone that neither harms nor benefits. Had I not seen the Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, kiss you, I would not have kissed you.”[4] These are ʿUmar's words, in which he reports an action of the Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace. We must therefore not attribute the entire statement to the Prophet as his own words.

This report combines following the prophetic example with rejecting belief in the stone itself as a source of benefit or harm. The reason ʿUmar explicitly gave for kissing it was to follow the Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace. This does not mean denying every merit accorded to the stone by Islamic law; otherwise, there would be no basis for following this example. Rather, the report shows that revering it is not the same as worshiping it or believing that it independently produces effects. Here, too, the limits of the argument become clear: the report clarifies the true nature of the Islamic practice and answers its portrayal as the worship of matter. On its own, however, it is not a proof that compels someone who denies prophethood to accept that the source of religious legislation is divine. That question is addressed through the evidence for prophethood and revelation. In this way, we preserve the force of the evidence within its proper scope, without requiring it to prove every disputed issue.

### Fifth: A Variety of Approaches Does Not Dispense with Evidence

The sacred has not been discussed from only one perspective. In *The Sacred and the Profane*, Mircea Eliade explains that an object which a religious person regards as a manifestation of the sacred remains, in its material aspect, the same object: a stone remains a stone, although its significance within religious experience differs.[5] This observation helps us understand that describing the material object and describing the experience do not answer the same question.

Eliade's approach, however, should not be taken as validating all religions or as automatically proving the Islamic conception. His ideas about the manifestation of the sacred do not necessarily correspond to the Islamic understanding of honor bestowed by Allah and the requirement of revealed authority. Nor does the existence of a scholar who disagrees with a sociological explanation justify claiming that all academic scholarship has rejected that explanation. What is required is to examine the argument, not to set one famous name against another.

We can therefore study how a Muslim learns to revere the Kaaba, and how the community and worship influence that person, while leaving the question of the source of this reverence open to examination in light of its evidence. Explaining the channels through which faith is transmitted is different from proving that its content is a human invention, and defending faith does not require denying those channels.

### A Summary for Young People

If you are told that society creates sacred things, establish what is meant: that people learn reverence from their environment, or that all religious sanctity is a baseless illusion? There is clear evidence for the first; the second requires further evidence. Read Durkheim faithfully, and do not attribute a merely descriptive account to him. Then examine his explanation without treating its author's fame as an argument. A Muslim's reverence for Allah's sacred rites and symbols rests on evidence from revelation. Feeling alone does not establish it, nor do differences in feeling invalidate it. Whoever carefully distinguishes between the substance of a belief and the way it is transmitted will avoid much of the confusion in this matter.

And all praise is due to Allah, Lord of the worlds.
