## The Balance Series: How to Weigh Doubts — Article Seven

## Events at the Kaaba and Its Enduring Sanctity

**In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful.**

Praise be to Allah, and blessings and peace be upon the Messenger of Allah, his family, his Companions, and those who follow him.

In the previous article, we discussed the distinction between a social explanation of reverence and the source of sanctity in Islam. Here we consider a question concerning the history of the Sacred House: How does the Kaaba remain sacred despite the fighting and bloodshed that have occurred in Mecca and its sanctuary? Does this conflict with the description of the House as a place to which people return and a place of safety?

This question may lead to another claim: Since Muslims continue to revere the House after those incidents, its sanctity must be merely a meaning that society creates, with no basis beyond its own feelings. Examining this claim, however, requires distinguishing three matters: establishing the historical events, understanding safety and sanctity in the scriptural texts, and determining what may legitimately be inferred from the continuation of reverence. Confusing these matters makes the events bear conclusions they do not support.

### First: Acknowledging the Events and Establishing Their Details

Mecca witnessed siege and fighting during the conflict between Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr and the Umayyad state. In his account of the events of 73 AH, Ibn Kathir mentions al-Hajjaj's siege of Mecca, the setting up of the catapult, stones striking the Kaaba, and the killing of Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr, may Allah be pleased with him and his father.[1] The person meant here is Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr, not his father, al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam. Getting the name right is necessary, although an error in it does not invalidate the underlying question about fighting in the sanctuary.

The account of Ibn al-Zubayr is not confined to lengthy historical narratives. Sahih Muslim contains Abu Nawfal's report of seeing Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr's body suspended, of Ibn Umar passing by it, and of what subsequently took place between al-Hajjaj and Asma bint Abi Bakr, may Allah be pleased with her.[2] Yet the established authenticity of this report does not mean that every detail related in accounts of the siege has the same standing. Each report has its own chain of transmission and must be assessed accordingly.

On the first of Muharram, 1400 AH, the armed assault on the Sacred Mosque took place. A commentary by Shaykh Abd al-Aziz ibn Baz, published in the year of the incident, documents the armed men's storming of the mosque, their gunfire, and their terrorizing of those present. It also includes the Council of Senior Scholars' statement concerning what happened.[3] There is no need to deny these events or minimize the harm people suffered in them in order to defend the sanctity of the House. Acknowledging the aggression is precisely what makes it possible to hold the aggressors accountable.

More precise language requires distinguishing the Kaaba from the Sacred Mosque surrounding it, and Mecca from its sanctuary. We should not relocate every event that occurred in one of these places to the interior of the Kaaba itself. This precision preserves historical accuracy without diminishing the inviolability of human life in any location.

### Second: Sanctity Does Not Mean That Aggression Is Impossible

For an act to be forbidden is one thing; for a person to be prevented from committing it by a barrier they cannot overcome is another. The inviolability of life does not mean that killing never happens, and the inviolability of property does not mean that theft is impossible. When a crime occurs, the ruling that identifies it as a crime remains in force. Aggression does not become permissible simply because the aggressor was able to carry it out.

The same applies to the sanctity of the sanctuary. It does not mean that human beings lose their ability to sin there, or that every attempted act of aggression will end in a miracle that prevents it from occurring. Rather, its sanctity requires that it be revered and that its violation be avoided, and it entails accountability for those who transgress. Bloodshed, therefore, does not necessarily mean that the place had no sanctity. Blood may be shed in violation of an established sanctity.

Conversely, it is also incorrect to say that a sinful act creates greater sanctity for a place.

The more precise formulation is that the sanctity of the place increases the gravity of the violation, and that aggression does not abolish that sanctity. The place's merit is established by its evidence, while liability attaches to the aggressor's act. These are two matters that must not be confused.

### Third: How Should We Understand the Description of the House as a Place of Safety?

Allah Most High says (a rendering of the meaning): “And when We made the House a place to which people return and a place of safety: ‘Take the Station of Abraham as a place of prayer.’ And We charged Abraham and Ishmael: ‘Purify My House for those who circle it, those who remain there in devotion, and those who bow and prostrate.’” [Al-Baqarah 2:125]

Safety here should not be reduced to an inward feeling unrelated to reality. The verse describes a blessing and a status bestowed upon the House, and reverence for the sanctuary has an effect on the safety of its inhabitants and visitors. In his commentary on the verse, Ibn Kathir brings together the description of the House in terms of both divine law and divine decree. Among the reports he mentions concerning reverence for it is that a man would encounter his father's killer there and leave him unharmed.[4]

This, however, is not equivalent to a promise that every individual in the sanctuary will be safe from every assault at every moment in history. That is an unwarranted generalization requiring a text to support it. The verse itself includes a command to Abraham and Ishmael, peace be upon them, to purify the House. It thus links the honor bestowed upon the place to what must be done there, rather than dispensing with obligation and action. This meaning becomes clear when the texts are considered together. In the context of fighting, Allah Most High says (a rendering of the meaning): “Kill them wherever you encounter them, and drive them out from where they drove you out, for persecution is worse than killing. Do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they fight you there. But if they fight you, then kill them. Such is the recompense of the disbelievers.” [Al-Baqarah 2:191]

The text that prohibits fighting at the Sacred Mosque mentions the possibility that the opposing side may initiate fighting there. This alone is enough to refute an interpretation that makes aggression in that place inherently impossible. The purpose here is not to set out the detailed rules governing warfare, but to show that the texts affirm both the sanctity of the place and the possibility that some people may violate it.

### Fourth: Evidence from the Sunnah During a Time of Conflict

Al-Bukhari relates that Abu Shurayh told Amr ibn Said, while the latter was dispatching troops to Mecca, that he had heard the Prophet, blessings and peace be upon him, say: “It was Allah who made Mecca sacred, not the people.” He then mentioned the prohibition on shedding blood there, explained that the permission given to the Prophet, blessings and peace be upon him, on the day of the conquest was exclusive to him, and stated that its sanctity had returned to what it had been before.[5]

This report brings together a scriptural text and an attempt to invoke it in a political situation. Abu Shurayh did not treat the movement of troops toward Mecca as evidence invalidating its sanctity. Rather, he recalled its sanctity so that it could serve as the standard for judging the act. The governor's reported response does not become a Prophetic statement merely because he uttered it, nor does it cancel the text conveyed by the Companion.

A practical consequence becomes apparent here: the continued sanctity of the place is not a justification for what happened there, but a basis for objecting to its violation. Protecting the status of the sanctuary does not mean exonerating everyone who fought there or shielding rulers' actions from criticism. It means judging those actions against the evidence while carefully verifying the details of each event.

### Fifth: What Does the Continuation of Reverence Establish?

The fact that the Kaaba remains revered after an assault does not, by itself, establish that its sanctity is a social construction. A Muslim explains this continued reverence by the continuing validity of the religious ruling, which the aggressors' actions have not abrogated. Anyone seeking to refute this explanation must therefore examine its source and evidence, rather than simply repeat the account of the assault.

Conversely, it is not sound to make the continuation of reverence an independent proof of its divine source, claiming that any socially created meaning would necessarily have collapsed at the first shock. Communities may preserve their symbols after attacks upon them; indeed, their attachment to those symbols may grow stronger. Persistence alone therefore does not settle the dispute over the source. That question must instead be addressed through the evidence relevant to it.

One may ask: Why was the House protected from the People of the Elephant, only for an assault upon it to occur later? The answer is that the account of protection in a particular event does not include a promise that the same form of protection will be repeated with every subsequent act of aggression. Nor does the absence of immediate punishment for an aggressor indicate Allah's approval of the act. We should not derive from a single event a rule that revelation has not stated, then treat history's failure to conform to that rule as an objection to revelation itself.

### A Summary for Young Readers

When incidents of fighting in the sanctuary are mentioned, do not deny those that are established, and do not confuse the Kaaba, the mosque, and the city. Then ask: Does the text establish that aggression is forbidden, or does it promise that aggression cannot occur? The former is not invalidated by the aggressor's disobedience. The safety of the House is both a blessing and a ruling to be safeguarded, not permission to neglect its protection. Continued reverence alone is insufficient to prove or disprove its source. Rather, the sanctity of the sanctuary is known through its evidence, and people's actions are weighed against it. Condemning aggression thus remains part of revering the House, not an objection to that reverence.

And praise be to Allah, Lord of all worlds.
