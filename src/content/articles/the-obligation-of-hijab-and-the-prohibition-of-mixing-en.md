## The Obligation of Hijab and the Prohibition of Mixing

## A Shar'i Grounding and an Account of Points of Agreement and Disagreement

The article opens by insisting that hijab and mixing between the sexes are not marginal questions. They belong to the very heart of the discussion of purity, chastity, the protection of honor, and the prevention of the causes of temptation and indecency. Neither topic can be studied properly in isolation from the other, because the Shari'ah does not treat indecency merely as a final act; it surrounds it with rulings and manners that keep people from approaching it, block the means to it, and guard hearts, eyes, and limbs.

The author notes that attempts to trivialize these rulings have multiplied, often by renaming them with modern labels that weaken their authority and sever texts from their purposes. Hijab has become, for some, a social custom rather than an act of worship, and the separation the Shari'ah requires between men and unrelated women is portrayed as rigidity. Yet gathering the texts, referring the ambiguous to the clear, and considering the understanding of the early generations and the scholars shows that the core of the matter is too clear to be diluted or doubted, even if some of its details remain open to ijtihad, which must not be widened until it demolishes the settled principle. The study therefore aims to show that the obligation of hijab in general is a manifest, settled ruling known necessarily in the religion; that the texts and general meanings of the Shari'ah condemn mixing and forbid its causes; and that the disagreement over covering the face and hands is real, with the stronger view being that they must be covered, while respecting that well-known juristic disagreement and not condemning anyone who holds a recognized scholarly view.

## The Unifying Principle

Hijab and the prohibition of mixing meet in one overarching principle: when Allah forbids indecency, He forbids its causes and means. This is evident in many texts, most clearly: "And do not approach unlawful sexual intercourse. Indeed, it is ever an immorality and is evil as a way" (al-Isra' 17:32), and "And do not approach immoralities - what is apparent of them and what is concealed" (al-An'am 6:151). The prohibition is not confined to the final act but extends to everything that leads to it or is feared to draw toward it. Under this heading fall displaying adornment, softness of speech, seclusion, unrestrained gazing, and mixing, especially the kind in which barriers weaken. Hijab is therefore not separate from the other rulings of chastity, and the discussion of mixing is not a purely social treatment detached from the foundations of the Shari'ah. Both branch from one purpose: protecting honor, guarding hearts, and building society on purity, chastity, and dignity.

## Hijab: What Is Agreed Upon and What Is Disputed

Sound method requires distinguishing the core ruling from disputed details. It is inaccurate to claim that every detail of hijab is a matter of consensus, and it is unfair to transfer disagreement over some of its limits to its very legitimacy. The position of the generality of scholars is that the basic obligation of hijab, and a woman's duty to cover her hair and body before unrelated men in general, is among the manifest, settled rulings of Islam known necessarily in the religion. The well-known juristic disagreement concerns certain details, chief among them the face and hands. Some contemporary discourse inflates the circle of disagreement and then uses it to weaken the core ruling, yet disagreement over particulars does not lift the general obligation. The precise statement is that hijab is a general Shar'i obligation known necessarily in the religion, that some of its questions are subject to well-known scholarly disagreement, and that this disagreement may not be used to tear down the foundation.

## Qur'anic Evidence for the Obligation of Hijab

**The verse of the head-coverings.** Allah says: "and not expose their adornment except that which [necessarily] appears thereof and to wrap [a portion of] their headcovers over their chests" (an-Nur 24:31). This is a foundational verse because it combines the prohibition of displaying adornment with the command to draw the *khimar* over the chest. The *khimar*, in its linguistic origin and usage, is what covers the head; commanding a woman to draw it over her chest indicates covering and concealment, not mere ornament or a symbolic form. Juristic precision requires noting that the phrase "except that which [necessarily] appears thereof" is the subject of well-known disagreement among scholars. It may not be ignored, but neither may it be stretched so far that it weakens the principle of covering established by the same verse.

**The verse of the outer garments.** Allah says: "O Prophet, tell your wives and your daughters and the women of the believers to bring down over themselves [part] of their outer garments. That is more suitable that they will be known and not be abused. And ever is Allah Forgiving and Merciful" (al-Ahzab 33:59). This verse is the foundation for a woman's dress when going out; it addresses the believing women generally, and its stated reason shows that the *jilbab* is not merely a social custom but a garment intended for covering, warding off harm, and protecting women from suspicion and degradation.

**The verse of elderly women.** Allah says: "And women of post-menstrual age who have no desire for marriage - there is no blame upon them for putting aside their outer garments [but] not displaying adornment. But to modestly refrain [from that] is better for them. And Allah is Hearing and Knowing" (an-Nur 24:60). The concession granted to women in whom the likelihood of temptation has ceased indicates that the rule for others is otherwise, and the verse's ending, that refraining is better for them, confirms that covering and chastity retain their purifying value even where some relief is granted.

**The verses on lowering the gaze.** Allah says: "Tell the believing men to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts. That is purer for them. Indeed, Allah is Acquainted with what they do" (an-Nur 24:30), and "And tell the believing women to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts" (an-Nur 24:31). These verses do not by themselves establish the details of hijab, but they lay down the principle that governs the whole subject: if the gaze itself must be restrained, then the Shari'ah establishes rulings that reduce what invites it and narrow the causes of temptation.

## Evidence from the Sunnah and the Jurists' Views on Covering the Face and Hands

To complete the study, the evidence of the Sunnah and the statements of the jurists must be added, because the most prominent point of disagreement is covering the face and hands, not hijab itself.

**The hadith of the woman in ihram and the niqab.** It is established in Sahih al-Bukhari (1838) and Sahih Muslim (1177), with slight variation, from Ibn 'Umar that the Prophet ﷺ said: "The woman in ihram should not wear the niqab, nor wear gloves." Those who hold that covering the face is obligatory argue that forbidding the niqab during ihram shows that covering the face was known among women outside ihram, since the Shari'ah does not forbid during a rite something that had no prior basis. The author considers this argument strong in meaning, and some infer from it that the face was customarily and religiously covered. Yet the hadith does not settle the dispute on its own, since the other side may say the prohibition concerns the niqab as a specific garment, not covering in general. It is therefore best described as supporting evidence for this view, not a decisive text that admits no other reading.

**The hadith of the Khath'ami woman and al-Fadl ibn 'Abbas.** In the account of the woman from Khath'am during the Farewell Pilgrimage, al-Fadl ibn 'Abbas began looking at her and she at him, and the Prophet ﷺ turned al-Fadl's face to the other side (reported by al-Bukhari, 1513, and Muslim, 1334). It is cited in two ways: the Prophet ﷺ did not approve of the gaze when a cause of temptation was present, and the face is usually the place of temptation, so the view that it must be covered is closer to the Shari'ah's purpose of blocking the means. Some who do not require covering the face cite the same hadith to show that the woman was not covering her face at the time, so it is a text over which arguments pull in both directions. What hardly anyone disputes is that it confirms the prohibition of the gaze that leads to temptation and supports caution in this area.

**The hadith of Umm Salamah circumambulating behind the people.** It is established in the two Sahihs that Umm Salamah said she complained to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ that she was unwell, and he said: "Circumambulate behind the people while you are riding" (reported by al-Bukhari, 1619, and Muslim, 1276). Although it concerns tawaf, its significance extends to a recognized principle: keeping distance between women and men when needed is an aim the Shari'ah observes, and the Shari'ah inclines to reducing crowding and closeness wherever possible.

**'A'ishah's tawaf after the revelation of hijab.** It is established in Sahih al-Bukhari that 'Ata' ibn Abi Rabah, asked how the wives of the Prophet ﷺ mixed with men during tawaf after hijab was prescribed, said: "They did not mix. 'A'ishah used to circumambulate apart from the men, not mixing with them" (reported by al-Bukhari, 1618). The author regards this as a very important text, explicit in denying mixing while affirming presence in the same general place, and so among the clearest proofs of the difference between a regulated gathering and blameworthy mixing.

**The jurists' views.** The disagreement over the face and hands is well known in the books of the schools. The Hanbali school, which is also the correct view according to a group of Shafi'is, holds that covering the face and hands before unrelated men is obligatory. In contrast, it is well known among many Hanafis and Malikis and a group of scholars that the face and hands are not 'awrah in principle, though they often restricted this to when there is no fear of temptation, and many of their later scholars stated that covering becomes obligatory when times are corrupt or temptation is feared. The question, therefore, is not a sharp opposition between one view permitting uncovering absolutely and another requiring covering absolutely; much of the jurists' discussion converges in practice on covering being preferable, and indeed required, when temptation prevails and the causes of corruption are present.

**The preferred view.** While acknowledging the well-known juristic disagreement, the author holds that covering the face and hands is the stronger view, the safer course for one's religious accountability, and the closer to the purposes of the Shari'ah, particularly in this age of widespread temptation, weakened religiosity, and expanded means of gazing, attachment, and allurement. This preference does not cancel the old disagreement or permit attacking anyone who follows a recognized scholarly view, provided they remain far from displaying adornment, from over-extending concessions, and from turning juristic disagreement into a pretext for discarding modesty and hijab altogether. The aim is to state the stronger view, not to restrict every scholar who differs or every person who follows a recognized view within its conditions.

## The Verse "From Behind a Partition" and the Limits of Its Use

Allah says: "ask them from behind a partition. That is purer for your hearts and their hearts" (al-Ahzab 33:53). This verse is important in establishing the principle of a Shar'i barrier, but using it accurately requires two observations. First, its immediate context concerns the Mothers of the Believers, and this must not be overlooked in interpretation. Second, the reason it gives, "That is purer for your hearts and their hearts," is general in meaning and shows that barriers and boundaries are more fitting for purity and further from suspicion. The verse is thus best used as evidence for the merit of Shar'i barriers and their effect on purifying hearts, rather than being made to carry, by itself and without the other evidence, every form of the subject.

## Defining the Point of Dispute on Mixing

Much confusion stems from the vagueness of the term "mixing." Not every gathering of men and women in one place falls under a single ruling, and not every shared presence in a street, mosque, season of worship, or public facility is the mixing the scholars condemn. The author defines Shar'i-blameworthy mixing as men and unrelated women coming together in a way that involves intermingling, weakened barriers, and expansive interaction, leading, or likely to lead, to forbidden gazing, emotional attachment, softness of speech, or familiarity contrary to modesty and dignity. What matters is not mere co-presence in a place but the manner of the gathering, its effects, and its outcomes. A distinction must be drawn between an incidental, regulated gathering called for by a genuine need or recognized benefit with Shar'i controls intact, and organized or extensive mixing that removes barriers, breaks reserve, and makes familiarity and attachment normal.

## The Difference Between Mixing and Seclusion

It is a mistake to confine the Shar'i prohibition to seclusion (*khalwah*) alone and then treat everything short of it as absolutely permissible. Seclusion is a specific matter explicitly forbidden by the texts: a man being alone with an unrelated woman in a place where they are safe from others' view, or where the sense of being alone that opens the door to suspicion is realized. Mixing is a different matter. Forbidden mixing can occur without seclusion if the environment is built on familiarity, weakened barriers, and repeated causes of temptation. Not all mixing is seclusion, and the absence of seclusion does not mean the absence of every form of corruption.

## General Evidence Against Mixing That Leads to Temptation

**The prohibition of approaching zina.** "And do not approach unlawful sexual intercourse" (al-Isra' 17:32) is a great principle here, since every form of gathering that breaks down modesty, opens the door to attachment, and multiplies glances and private conversations falls within the general meaning the Shari'ah came to keep away.

**The prohibition of seclusion and of entering upon women.** The Sunnah explicitly forbids a man's seclusion with an unrelated woman and warns against entering upon women, confirming that the Shari'ah does not wait for indecency to occur but begins by preventing its preliminaries. The Prophet ﷺ said: "A woman should not travel except with a mahram, and no man should enter upon her unless she has a mahram with her." A man said: "O Messenger of Allah, I want to go out with such-and-such army, and my wife wants to perform Hajj." He said: "Go with her" (reported by al-Bukhari, 1862, and Muslim, 1341, with slight variation). He also said: "No man should be alone with a woman unless she has a mahram with her, and a woman should not travel except with a mahram." A man stood and said: "O Messenger of Allah, my wife has gone out for Hajj, and I have been enlisted in such-and-such expedition." He said: "Go and perform Hajj with your wife" (reported by Muslim, 1341, and al-Bukhari, 3006, with slight variation). And he said: "Beware of entering upon women." A man from the Ansar said: "O Messenger of Allah, what about the in-law (*al-hamw*)?" He said: "The in-law is death" (reported by al-Bukhari, 5232, Muslim, 2172, and al-Tirmidhi, 1171, in their wording).

**Separation in the rows of prayer.** In places of worship, the Shari'ah observed a separation that wards off temptation. Muslim reports in his Sahih from Abu Hurayrah that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "The best rows for men are the first and the worst are the last, and the best rows for women are the last and the worst are the first." The hadith was also reported by Ahmad and the authors of the four Sunan. All this indicates that reducing contact is a protection intended by the Shari'ah, not a passing social arrangement.

**Lowering the gaze and guarding chastity.** The command to lower the gaze is not merely an individual duty; it also shows that the Shari'ah does not accept creating environments that make this duty extremely difficult and a constant trial. Expanding organized forms of interaction that prepare the way for gazing, conversation, and attachment therefore contradicts the purpose of these texts.

**The story of Yusuf and the account of the two daughters of Shu'ayb.** The story of Yusuf, peace be upon him, shows the danger of a gathering fraught with temptation when it lacks protective barriers, and the account of the two daughters of Shu'ayb, peace be upon him, shows the character of keeping away from crowding with men at a place of need. The point drawn from both is the general meaning that the Shari'ah inclines toward covering, dignity, and distance, not toward intermingling and breaking down barriers.

## The Objection from Mixing During Hajj

A recurring objection cites women performing tawaf alongside men during Hajj to argue that mixing as such was absolutely permissible. The author rejects this on two grounds. Methodologically, events that preceded the settling of certain rulings cannot be set against later, settled texts; what happened before hijab was prescribed cannot be used as proof against what was established after it. As for the authentic hadiths, they do not show permission to intermingle but rather presence in the same general place while maintaining distance and avoiding crowding as far as possible. 'Ata's report in al-Bukhari, quoted above, explicitly denies mixing. In the same narration, a woman said to 'A'ishah, "Let us go and touch [the Black Stone], O Mother of the Believers," and she refused, saying, "Go by yourself." This shows that attaining some secondary merits does not take precedence over preserving dignity and avoiding crowding. It is supported by the hadith of Umm Salamah in the two Sahihs, "Circumambulate behind the people while you are riding," another text establishing distance rather than expanded mixing. Men and women being in the same general place during Hajj, a seasonal act of worship in which large gatherings are hard to avoid entirely, does not entail the permissibility of blameworthy mixing. Even there the Shari'ah did not drop the principle of chastity, but maintained it through avoiding intermingling, keeping distance where possible, not crowding, and giving priority to safety from temptation.

## Applying the Aims of Chastity in Contemporary Work and Study

The study should not stop at abstract grounding but must include the jurisprudence of application, since many people live in educational or professional settings where some gathering of men and women is imposed, and they need a disciplined Shar'i account that neither dissolves the ruling nor ignores reality. The principle is that an obligation does not lapse because one cannot achieve its most complete form; rather, the duty shifts to realizing its purpose as far as one is able. If the ideal environment free of mixing cannot be reached, this must not become a reason to trivialize the ruling or redefine the wrong. One must strive to reduce the violation and its effects, multiply safeguards, and build feasible alternatives. Key safeguards include maintaining Shar'i hijab, lowering the gaze, controlling speech, avoiding softness of speech, preventing familiarity beyond need, avoiding seclusion and what resembles it, confining the relationship to the professional or academic framework, staying away from private messages, personal joking, and side meetings, reducing unnecessary mixed meetings, and seeking alternatives further from temptation whenever possible.

## Prominent Contemporary Objections

**That hijab is custom, not worship.** This claim does not accord with the texts or with the practice and jurisprudence of the Ummah across the centuries. Customs may affect some forms and styles of dress, but the basic covering of women, the prohibition of displaying adornment, and the command concerning the *khimar* and *jilbab* are not matters referred to mere custom.

**That preventing mixing paralyzes life.** This objection frames the question as a choice between unrestrained, extensive mixing and the complete paralysis of life, which is a false framing. The Shari'ah does not forbid legitimate interests; it commands pursuing them in a disciplined way, reducing the causes of temptation and limiting interaction to what is needed.

**That prevailing reality indicates permissibility.** The spread of a violation does not change the ruling. Forbidden things may become widespread in some eras while their ruling remains as it is; truth is known not by majorities but by revelation and its correct understanding.

**That self-confidence removes temptation.** The author calls this one of the weakest arguments, because the Shari'ah was not built on souls vouching for themselves, but on knowing their fickleness, susceptibility to temptation, weakness, and haste. That is why it prevents the causes rather than relying on bare exhortation while leaving the doors open.

## The Da'wah and Educational Dimension

Presented without its spirit of da'wah and education, this subject may appear to some as a set of dry restrictions. In truth, the Shari'ah prescribed these rulings only out of mercy for people, to protect hearts, preserve homes, and guard honor. Hijab is not merely clothing but a sign of chastity, dignity, and submission to Allah's command, and preventing mixing is not a constraint on people but a protection from the spread of relationships unregulated by the Shari'ah, whose effects then reach families, souls, and consciences. Part of complete da'wah here is not to present the ruling as hostility toward women or as a cancellation of life's needs, but in its true form: a ruling that protects both men and women and establishes their dealings on seriousness and dignity rather than casual familiarity and dilution.

## Conclusion

The study concludes that hijab in general is a manifest, settled Shar'i obligation known necessarily in the religion, while the recognized disagreement remains over some of its limits and details, foremost the covering of the face and hands. Mixing in which barriers weaken, causes of temptation multiply, and harm prevails or its outcome is feared is forbidden in the Shari'ah, because when Allah forbids indecency He forbids its causes and means. The Prophetic Sunnah and the statements of the jurists reinforce this principle; the most prominent disagreement concerns the face and hands, not hijab itself; and the stronger view is that they must be covered, especially in times of widespread temptation, while the well-known juristic disagreement remains and those who follow a recognized scholarly view are not to be dismissed or condemned. The events of Hajj do not stand as a counterargument, since the authentic hadiths do not approve blameworthy intermingling but show general presence with separation, distance, and avoidance of crowding as far as possible, confirming that even where full separation is difficult, the Shari'ah keeps the principle of chastity preserved according to capacity.

What is required today, the author writes, is not to apologize for Allah's rulings or reshape them to suit the pressure of reality, but to understand them correctly, apply them wisely, and strive to realize their aims as far as possible, for there is no soundness of hearts, safety of honor, or uprightness of society except by keeping Allah's limits, honoring His commands, and closing the doors that lead to temptation and corruption. The article closes with the report of 'Abd Allah ibn 'Abbas that Abu Sufyan told him Heraclius said to him: "I asked you what he commands you, and you said that he commands you to pray, to be truthful, to be chaste, to fulfill covenants, and to render trusts. This is the description of a prophet" (reported by al-Bukhari, 2681, and Muslim, 1773, with similar wording).
